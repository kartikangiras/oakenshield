Can an AI playbook beat a coin flip?

Oakenshield is a release process for decision logic written by AI. An agent writes the change, a Flagsmith flag holds it, a randomised experiment judges it on paper and a person approves each step. The proof runs live: two AI written trading playbooks against a coin.

The promotion ladder

A release process for decisions an AI wrote

An agent can write a new rule in a minute and argue well for it. Before that rule decides anything, it climbs one rung at a time, and a person approves each climb. The arena is the second rung, run in the open.

  1. 1

    Backtest

    Each playbook is replayed on past markets. The agents saw 48 hours of them, and the newest 24 hours were held back and scored afterwards. A good backtest earns a place in the experiment and nothing more.

    Built
  2. 2

    Paper experiment

    One Flagsmith flag holds the versions. Flagsmith deals every live market to one of them at random, and a rule fixed before the start reads the result once. The fills come from real order books, and no money moves.

    The arena
  3. 3

    Small canary

    With real stakes, the winner would next decide a small share of the traffic under the same loss limit. This project stops before that rung: everything here is paper, so there is no small real exposure to take.

    Not built
  4. 4

    Full rollout

    A person approves. Flagsmith completes the experiment with the winner and the flag serves it to every market in that environment. Here that is still paper trading.

    Built, on paper

Beating the coin is not making money: a playbook also has to pay for the spread and the fee. A win on one rung earns the next rung, not real money.

How it works

An argument about trading, settled like any other experiment

An agent can write a trading playbook in a minute and argue well for it. A good backtest does not settle whether it works, so the arena hands the question to live markets and to a rule written down before the first one.

  1. 1

    The agents write playbooks

    Two agents get the same fact sheet and opposite convictions: one trades with the recent move, the other against it. Each writes its rules, a hypothesis and a prediction, attacks the other's playbook and may revise its own once.

  2. 2

    Flagsmith deals the markets

    Every five minutes each of eight crypto assets gets a new market. One flag with three versions decides who trades it: the coin, Agent A or Agent B. The trade is filled on paper and settled by the market's real resolution.

  3. 3

    A rule decides, a person approves

    The verdict is read once, when every version has its 240 markets, by a rule fixed before the start. Nothing starts and nothing rolls out without a person's yes, typed on their own terminal.

The eight steps, and who does each

  1. 1The briefA personWrites one goal, the event that counts, the venue, the paper stake and the rule.
  2. 2Two playbooksThe agentsEach gets the same fact sheet on 4,608 past markets: how often Up won, split by each of ten price signals. Each writes rules over those signals, a hypothesis in one sentence and a prediction.
  3. 3Cross examinationThe agentsEach attacks the other's playbook and may revise its own once. The newest 2,304 markets are held back from both and scored afterwards.
  4. 4Start approvedA personNothing exists in Flagsmith until now. The person types the environment's name on their own terminal and passes an approval code.
  5. 5The dealFlagsmithOne flag, three versions. For every live market the runner calls getExperimentFlag with the market as the identity, and Flagsmith answers who trades it.
  6. 6Paper tradesThe runnerMakes that version's call, fills it from the real order book and settles it when Polymarket resolves the market. Each outcome goes to Flagsmith as an event.
  7. 7The verdictThe refereeRead once, when every version has its planned sample. "Neither beats the coin" is a valid answer.
  8. 8Rollout approvedA personA second yes. Flagsmith completes the experiment with the winner, and the flag serves it to every market in that environment.

The start, the verdict and the rollout are the flagsmith-duel skill's script, which reaches Flagsmith's experiments API through the Flagsmith CLI. The runner reads the flag and sends its events with the Flagsmith SDK.

The experiment

Momentum against reversal, with a coin in between

The venue is Polymarket's five minute Up or Down markets on eight crypto assets. A market resolves Up when the price at the end of its five minutes is at or above the price at the start. Counting the spread and the fee, a trader breaks even at about 53 percent right calls.

Three guards

Three guards keep the run honest

An arena can go wrong in three places: in what an agent writes, in Flagsmith while the experiment runs, and in which markets get counted. Each has its own guard.

  • Before the start

    The playbook referee

    A playbook is data, not code: at most 8 rules over ten price signals. The referee runs each one on the past markets and rejects it when it says the same side more than 70% of the time or has a rule that fired fewer than 5 times.

  • During the run, in Flagsmith

    The seal and the audit

    The start writes a seal: the experiment, the split, the metrics and a hash of every version's contents. The audit compares Flagsmith with the seal and reports a changed split, an edited, deleted, added or renamed version, changed metrics, a changed audience or a pause.

  • During the run, in the runner

    No say in who trades what

    A market is dropped for missing prices or a thin order book before any version is asked for it, so a skip cannot favour one. Once Flagsmith has named a version, the market counts whatever happens.

Why the seal exists

We expected Flagsmith to lock a running experiment. When we tested Flagsmith Cloud on 27 November 2026, its Admin API accepted a change of split, an edit to a version's value and the deletion of a version while an experiment was running, and a paused experiment went on serving every version. So the lock is the skill's own: the verdict and the rollout refuse while the seal is broken.

Controls while it trades

Loss limit
Once the last 100 settled markets have lost 600 paper dollars between them, every version stops. All stop together, so the stop cannot favour one.
Restart by a person
A stop outlasts a restart of the runner. Trading resumes only when a person types the environment's name in their own terminal.
Heartbeat
The runner reports at least once a minute. After three quiet minutes the dashboard says when it was last heard.
Fixed sample, read once
240 markets per version, set before the start. The result is read once, so nobody can stop on a lucky streak.
Caps on every market
$10 of paper money a market. A market is skipped when a side costs more than 0.65 a share or the order book is too thin for the stake.
Break even beside the win rate

Experiment Guard protects the code an experiment depends on

It reads the experiments running in Flagsmith, reads the pull request, and blocks the merge when the change breaks what an experiment measures or serves. It has thirteen rules, and only the provable ones block.

Agent Skill

The same job for any coding agent and any flag

flagsmith-duel is the Flagsmith half of both experiments as an Agent Skill: one job from plan to rollout, on the Flagsmith CLI. A version's value is any text or JSON, so it settles a disagreement about a message, a price, a screen or a trading playbook the same way.

/plugin install flagsmith-duel --marketplace kartikangiras/oakenshield

Works in Claude Code 2.1.275 or later. Needs the Flagsmith CLI 2.0 or later, Node 20 or later and git.

FAQ

Questions worth asking first

Does it trade real money?

No. Every trade is on paper: $10 a market, priced from the asks in Polymarket's real order book with the market's fee, and settled by the market's real resolution. No order is ever placed, and there is no account, wallet or key.

Is this only for trading?

No. Trading is the proof because a market answers in five minutes. The flag, the experiment, the guards and the approvals do not depend on what the versions decide. The ladder lists pricing rules, fraud thresholds, prompt or model changes and ranking as other fits. None of those has been run with it yet.

What stops a playbook that goes badly wrong?

A loss limit. Once the last 100 settled markets have lost 600 paper dollars between them, the runner opens nothing for any version, and it stays stopped until a person restarts it from their own terminal. The limit counts every version together, so it cannot tilt the experiment.

Why is a coin the control?

Because it is the honest baseline. Both sides of a market cost about 50 cents a share when the trade is made, so a trader with no edge is right about half the time. The coin's call is one bit of a hash of a published seed and the market's name, so anyone can check it.

Where do the prices come from?

Markets, order books and resolutions come from Polymarket's public data. The price signals a playbook reads come from Binance's public market data. All of it is read without an account.

What is one unit of the experiment?

One market: one asset in one five minute window. Its name is the identity Flagsmith randomises, so Flagsmith decides who trades it, records the exposure and counts the outcome. The runner has no say.

What if neither playbook beats the coin?

Then the coin stays. That is a valid answer, and the rule says so before the start. A playbook qualifies only above a 95% chance to beat the coin on the goal, with its guardrail holding.

Do the agents have access to Flagsmith or to the markets?

No. The two agents run with no tools and hold no credentials. Each one's whole output is one JSON object, a playbook, that the referee parses. The runner applies it, and every read and write of the experiment goes through one script on the Flagsmith CLI.

What stops an agent from starting or rolling out on its own?

The start and the rollout each need an approval code, which is a digest of what the person was shown. The arena's environment is protected, so the person also confirms on their own terminal by typing the environment's name. These approvals are a convention for an agent that follows its instructions. Against one that does not, keep the Flagsmith key out of its reach.

Does Flagsmith lock a running experiment?

Not when we tested Flagsmith Cloud on 27 November 2026. Its Admin API accepted a change of split, an edit to a version's value and the deletion of a version during a run. That is why the start writes a seal and the audit checks Flagsmith against it.

What does it need to run?

The arena needs the Flagsmith CLI 2.0, a Flagsmith organisation API key, Node 22.18 or newer, and Claude Code logged in for the two agents. The skill on its own needs the Flagsmith CLI 2.0 or later, Node 20 or later and git.

What does this site record about me?

This page sends no events and keeps nothing in your browser. It reads the arena's score from this site's own server. The dashboard keeps a random ID in your browser. If you run a check or copy a setup snippet there, that event goes to Flagsmith with the ID. Only a visitor who opens the dashboard with no page in the link is entered in an experiment, and every link on this page opens a named page.

Is it free and open source?

Yes. Oakenshield is open source under the MIT license. The code, the brief, the whole debate and the files of the run are in the repository on GitHub.
